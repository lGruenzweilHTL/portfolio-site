Story point scale (modified Fibonacci)

Scrum typically uses Planning Poker with a modified Fibonacci sequence to estimate relative effort/complexity in Story Points:

Values
0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20, 40, 100
Why not absolute time (hours/days)?
  1. Different developers work at different speeds (a senior might take 2h, a junior 8h) — but the story's relative complexity stays the same regardless of who estimates.
  2. Story points capture inherent risk, uncertainty, and complexity better than a raw time figure.
  3. Stakeholders tend to misread absolute time estimates as binding deadlines.
Planning poker procedure
  1. Each member gets a card deck
  2. Team picks a story to estimate
  3. Product Owner explains it until everyone understands
  4. Everyone privately selects a card, places it face-down
  5. All cards flip simultaneously
  6. If all match → estimate is recorded, move on
  7. If not → highest and lowest estimator explain their reasoning, then re-estimate
Why this process matters

The core value is discussion, not the number itself. Showing cards simultaneously prevents anchoring on the first person's guess. Disagreement forces the team to surface hidden assumptions or unclear scope before work starts.

Periodically re-check older estimates against newer ones to keep the scale internally consistent across the whole backlog.

Definition of Done (DoD)Acceptance Criteria (AC)
ScopeApplies to every story / the whole incrementSpecific to one story
Defined byThe development team, agreed once for the whole productProduct Owner (in collaboration with the team), per story
AnswersIs the increment production-ready as a whole? (tests pass, code reviewed, docs updated, ...)Does this specific story meet the required functional behavior?

The four paired levels

The V-Modell pairs each development phase on the left side with a corresponding test phase on the right side — defined together, not as an afterthought:

Left (Spec / Design)↔Right (Test)
System Specification
(Pflichtenheft, Use-Case-Diagramm)		↔Abnahmetest / Akzeptanztest
durchgeführt gemeinsam mit dem Kunden
Design (Grobentwurf)
Schnittstellen, Package Diagram, Deployment Diagram		↔Systemtest
Gesamtsystem getestet, z.B. GUI, REST-Schnittstellen
Design (Feinentwurf)
Klassendiagramm		↔Integrationstest
Zusammenspiel verschiedener Komponenten
Implementierung↔Modultest / Unittest
nur Methoden einer Klasse; jUnit, AssertJ, Mockito
What the horizontal connections mean Each left-side step and its right-side partner are planned at the same level of detail, simultaneously — e.g. acceptance tests are derived directly from the System Specification, before implementation even starts. This enables preventive testing: logic errors surface early instead of only at the very end (as in plain Waterfall).
Verifikation vs. Validierung
VerifikationValidierung
Question"Mache ich die Dinge richtig?""Mache ich die richtigen Dinge?"
Checks againstThe design/specification itselfThe actual customer need
Applies to (in this V-Modell)The three lower levels: Systemtest, Integrationstest, UnittestOnly the top level: Abnahmetest, performed with the customer
Why only the top level is Validierung Only the Akzeptanztest directly checks the result against what the customer actually wanted. The three lower test levels verify that the system matches its own internal design/specification — correct relative to the design, which is not the same as correct relative to the customer's real need.
Classic "Big-Bang" V-Modell vs. V-Modell with Scrum
Big-Bang-Approach (classic)V-Modell mit Scrum (agile)
How often the V runsOnce, for the entire systemRepeatedly — one small V per sprint
Output per cycleThe whole product, at the very endAn MVP (Minimal Viable Product) after every sprint
Feedback timingOnly after the single full V completesContinuous — every sprint produces a testable increment

The V-Modell's core idea (pairing each design step with its matching test level) isn't exclusive to classic processes — Scrum can apply the same V-shaped discipline at a much smaller scale, once per sprint, instead of once for the whole project.

Comparison
Classic (Waterfall / V-Modell Big-Bang)Agile (Scrum, V-Modell per sprint)
RequirementsFixed early, in detail, before implementationCaptured incrementally, refined throughout the project

Why agile models emerged

Classic models assume requirements can be fully understood and frozen up front. In practice this is often unrealistic — agile models were developed specifically to react more flexibly to changing requirements, by shortening the feedback loop between "build something" and "find out if it's right".
